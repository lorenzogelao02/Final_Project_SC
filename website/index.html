Explore the Story

A City of Contrast

New York City contains immense wealth, infrastructure, and public services. At the same time, thousands of people without stable housing must navigate the city day by day to find basic necessities: clean water, toilets, safe places to rest, connectivity, and support services.

This project asks a simple question: where in NYC is it easiest — or hardest — to access the resources needed to get through the day during periods of urban stress?

The Data Behind the Project

We combine public datasets from NYC Open Data to study survival-related infrastructure: resources that can help people meet immediate needs in public space.

Drop-in Centers

Locations offering meals, showers, medical help, and rest space.

Public Toilets

Operational automatic public toilets across the city.

Drinking Fountains

Outdoor fountains in NYC parks with geographic coordinates.

LinkNYC Kiosks

Free Wi-Fi, charging, calls, 911 button, and city service access.

Shelter Repair Scorecard

Shelter buildings and reported repair violations.

RHY Daily Census

Daily counts of beds and vacancies for runaway and homeless youth services.

Drug-related Crime

NYPD reports used as a local risk indicator.

Defining the Survival Score

The Survival Score is a spatial accessibility model designed to estimate how well different parts of New York City are supported by nearby survival-related public infrastructure. Rather than measuring homelessness directly, the score evaluates how accessible essential urban resources are within the surrounding area.

The model combines spatial proximity to public resources such as drinking fountains, public toilets, LinkNYC kiosks, and drop-in centers, while also accounting for nearby environmental strain through drug-related crime incidents.

Survival Score = Resource Accessibility − Environmental Risk

Nearby infrastructure increases the score, while surrounding crime exposure reduces it. Each resource type contributes differently based on its potential importance for short-term urban survivability.

Resource Accessibility

The model evaluates proximity to drinking fountains, public toilets, LinkNYC hubs, and drop-in centers. Areas with denser infrastructure clusters receive higher scores, reflecting stronger access to essential urban support systems.

Environmental Risk

Nearby drug-related crime incidents are incorporated as indicators of environmental strain and vulnerability. Higher concentrations of nearby incidents reduce the overall Survival Score.

Weighted Spatial Model

Different infrastructure types are weighted differently within the score calculation. Drop-in centers and public toilets contribute more heavily than fountains or Wi-Fi hubs due to their broader support value, while crime exposure introduces a spatial penalty factor.

What the Data Reveals

When the datasets are combined into a single geographic system, clear spatial inequalities begin to appear across New York City. Essential survival resources, as drinking water, toilets, shelters, internet access, and emergency support services are not distributed evenly throughout the urban landscape.

Some neighborhoods contain dense networks of public infrastructure, where multiple resources can be reached within only a few minutes. Other areas contain large gaps between services, forcing vulnerable individuals to travel significantly farther to access basic needs. For people living in unstable conditions, these distances can become critical barriers affecting safety, health, communication, hygiene, and access to support systems.

The analysis also reveals that resource accessibility and urban risk often overlap in complex ways. Certain parts of the city provide relatively strong access to support infrastructure while simultaneously showing high concentrations of drug related crime. Other areas suffer from both low resource density and increased exposure to risk, producing some of the lowest Survival Scores in the city.

The project demonstrates that survival in a city is not only about whether resources exist overall, but whether they are realistically accessible within the spaces people move through every day. Even in a city with thousands of public services, accessibility remains highly uneven.

Resource density is unequal

Public infrastructure is heavily concentrated in certain parts of Manhattan and central urban areas, while outer neighborhoods often contain significantly fewer nearby resources. This creates major differences in accessibility depending on location.

Distance directly affects survival

Access depends not only on how many resources exist, but also on whether they are reachable within a reasonable distance. Long distances between water, toilets, shelters, or support centers can make daily survival substantially more difficult for vulnerable populations.

Risk and support coexist

Some areas with strong support infrastructure also experience higher concentrations of crime indicators. This creates environments where access to help exists, but exposure to danger remains elevated at the same time.

Survival is spatial

The project highlights how geography itself shapes opportunity, safety, and access to basic human needs. A few blocks in the city can dramatically change the availability of services and the overall Survival Score of an area.

Limitations

The Survival Score is not a direct measure of homelessness or lived experience. It does not capture opening hours, crowding, safety perception, accessibility, service quality, policing, weather, or whether someone is actually allowed to use a resource.

Still, it helps reveal an important urban question: where does the city provide enough basic infrastructure, and where might vulnerable people face longer distances between essential services?
