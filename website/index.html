A City of Contrast

New York City contains immense wealth, infrastructure, and public services. At the same time, thousands of people without stable housing must navigate the city day by day to find basic necessities: clean water, toilets, safe places to rest, connectivity, and support services.

This project asks a simple question: where in NYC is it easiest — or hardest — to access the resources needed to get through the day?

The Data Behind the Project

We combine public datasets from NYC Open Data to study survival-related infrastructure: resources that can help people meet immediate needs in public space.

Drop-in Centers

Locations offering meals, showers, medical help, and rest space.

Public Toilets

Operational automatic public toilets across the city.

Drinking Fountains

Outdoor fountains in NYC parks with geographic coordinates.

LinkNYC Kiosks

Free Wi-Fi, charging, calls, 911 button, and city service access.

Shelter Repair Scorecard

Shelter buildings and reported repair violations.

RHY Daily Census

Daily counts of beds and vacancies for runaway and homeless youth services.

Drug-related Crime

NYPD reports used as a local risk indicator.

Defining the Survival Score

The Survival Score is a spatial accessibility index. It does not measure homelessness directly. Instead, it estimates how well different parts of NYC are served by nearby survival-related resources while accounting for nearby risk indicators.

Survival Score = 70% Resource Access − 30% Risk Exposure

Resource access increases with proximity to drinking fountains, public toilets, drop-in centers, and LinkNYC kiosks. Risk exposure increases with nearby drug-related crime hotspots and shelter quality issues.

Access factors

Water, toilets, Wi-Fi, charging, drop-in centers, and other public support infrastructure.

Risk factors

Drug-related crime hotspots and shelter repair violations used as indicators of local strain.

What the Data Reveals

Survival infrastructure is not evenly distributed. Some areas have dense clusters of public amenities, while others have large gaps between basic services. This means that a person’s ability to access help may depend heavily on where they are in the city.

Resource density is unequal

Public amenities are concentrated in specific parts of the city, creating stronger access in some neighborhoods and weaker access in others.

Distance matters

A city can contain many resources overall while still leaving specific areas underserved if resources are too far apart.

Risk and access overlap

Some locations combine lower access to basic resources with higher exposure to risk indicators, producing lower Survival Scores.

Limitations

The Survival Score is not a direct measure of homelessness or lived experience. It does not capture opening hours, crowding, safety perception, accessibility, service quality, policing, weather, or whether someone is actually allowed to use a resource.

Still, it helps reveal an important urban question: where does the city provide enough basic infrastructure, and where might vulnerable people face longer distances between essential services?
